Lewis structure recipe
  1. Count valence electrons. Add electrons for a negative charge; subtract for a positive charge.
  2. Choose the skeleton. H is terminal; the less electronegative atom is often central.
  3. Add single bonds, using two electrons per bond.
  4. Complete terminal octets, then place remaining electrons on central atoms.
  5. Make multiple bonds if needed. Check the electron budget and formal charges.

Bond & molecular polarity

Use the electronegativities from your yellow sheet. For this game's classification convention:

  • ΔEN ≤ 0.4: nonpolar covalent
  • ΔEN > 0.4: polar covalent for the molecular bonds in this practice bank.

The cutoff is an approximate teaching guideline. A large ΔEN can suggest ionic character, but it does not by itself make a substance ionic: HF and BF₃ are covalent molecules. “Nonpolar” at the cutoff means approximately nonpolar.

Bond dipoles point toward the higher EN atom. Use the 3D molecular geometry to decide if the dipoles cancel. CO₂ has polar bonds but is a nonpolar molecule.

Periodic-trend polarity guide

Electronegativity (EN) increases up a group and left to right across a period. The atom closer to fluorine in the upper right corner pulls bonding electrons harder, so it becomes the δ− end.

  • Same atom on both ends: nonpolar bond.
  • F, O, and N bonded to C or H: polar bonds.
  • C–H and C–C: treated as nonpolar.
  • More polar bond: the one whose partner atoms are farther apart on the table.

Trend rules are a shortcut. A few pairs (such as H with Be, Al, or Si) break the pattern, so those are not used here. A polar bond still needs an uneven 3D shape to make a polar molecule.

Octet exceptions (CHEM 1A)
  • H: a duet (2 electrons).
  • Electron-deficient: Be often has 4; B often has 6. CH₃⁺ also has 6 around carbon.
  • Odd-electron species: NO and NO₂ contain an unpaired electron.
  • Expanded valence: period 3 and heavier central atoms can be drawn with more than 8 electrons in introductory Lewis structures.

C, N, O, and F do not expand their octets.

Resonance (CHEM 1A)

Keep the atoms and their connections fixed. Move electron pairs and change bond orders. The real species is a resonance hybrid; it does not switch between separate structures.

Organic structure toolbox (CHEM 3B)
  1. Expanded: draw every atom and bond. Lewis drawings also show lone pairs.
  2. Condensed: collect attached H with each atom: CH₃CH₂OH. Parentheses attach branches to the preceding atom: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃.
  3. Skeletal: unlabeled corners and ends are C; C-bound H is implied. Label O, N, S, halogens, and the H attached to them.

Carbon: four bonds total (single = 1, double = 2, triple = 3). A carbon with one single bond is CH₃; two single bonds is CH₂; a double and a single is CH.

Carbonyl: C(=O), also abbreviated CO; CHO is an aldehyde, COOH a carboxylic acid, and COO between carbon groups an ester. “CO” here means a carbonyl within a condensed formula, not the separate molecule carbon monoxide.

iso: a common branched arrangement such as (CH₃)₂CH–. sec: attachment through a secondary carbon, CH₃CH(–)CH₂CH₃. tert: attachment through a tertiary carbon, (CH₃)₃C–. These are common-name fragments, not universal replacement prefixes for IUPAC names.

Phenyl: C₆H₅ or Ph means a benzene ring attached through one carbon. Use the aromatic-ring stamp, then add its substituent.

VSEPR is local: analyze one atom at a time. Amide and aniline nitrogens involve delocalization and are omitted from simple local VSEPR questions. Sulfur and conformation-dependent aromatic examples are omitted from the introductory polarity set.
